Reviewer 1: 
 
1. General Comments and Suggestions:: 
- The study presents the design of a low-cost, webcam-based eye-tracking system capable of 
analyzing reader preferences. Beyond this primary application, the proposed system is 
structured to be extensible, allowing integration into various analytical frameworks and problem 
domains. 
 
In the study, the MediaPipe library was employed for iris tracking. To mitigate noise arising from 
micro-movements, a smoothing approach based on the Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) method 
was implemented. The extracted eye coordinates were subsequently transmitted to other web-
based application layers, and satisfactory performance outcomes were achieved. 
 
From a writing perspective, the manuscript is well structured and demonstrates an adequate 
literature review. Nevertheless, several issues requiring clarification, revision, or further 
discussion are outlined below: 
 
1. The system operates following a 20-second calibration procedure. After calibration, is the user 
required to maintain a fixed head position, or is head displacement tolerated? Specifically, how 
does the system perform when the user shifts laterally from the center of the screen? The 
robustness of the model against head movement should be clarified and, if possible, 
quantitatively evaluated. 
 
2. It appears that the KDE method is applied to temporal gaze points. This should be explained 
more explicitly in the manuscript. Updating Figure 4 to better illustrate this process would 
improve clarity. Furthermore, does KDE provide a measurable performance advantage 
compared to simply computing the centroid (mean) of the gaze points? This comparison should 
be analyzed and discussed. For example, if a computationally simpler averaging algorithm 
achieves comparable performance while reducing processing time by even a few milliseconds, it 
may represent a more efficient alternative. 
 
Additionally, while KDE appears to stabilize (fix) the eye center, this may introduce a potential 
limitation. For instance, could such smoothing mask indicators of attentional instability in 
individuals with focus-related difficulties? In other words, might this approach inadvertently 
suppress diagnostically relevant variability? This issue could be addressed in the discussion 
section.

Reviewer 2: 
 
The manuscript presents a practically motivated, end-to-end system for real-time, browser-
based reading analysis using webcam-derived gaze signals and paragraph-level dwell-time 
analytics, complemented by optional AI-assisted support (e.g., summarization and text-to-
speech). The integration of a Chrome extension with a companion service and an interactive UI 
is a clear strength and suggests potential real-world applicability. However, in its current form 
the paper does not yet meet publication standards due to critical gaps in ethics/privacy and 
consent, study protocol transparency, baseline/ablation evidence, metric/ground-truth clarity, 
and real-time performance reporting. The issues below must be addressed. 
 
1) Ethics, informed consent, and data privacy protocol is missing / inconsistent 
 
The study relies on webcam-based eye tracking and derives gaze-related signals from face/eye 
landmarks, which may involve biometric and/or personally sensitive data. However, the 
manuscript states that ethics approval is not required; in its current form, this justification is 
insufficient and high-risk for a human-subject study. 
 
Missing: A verifiable ethics/consent/privacy and data governance description. 
 
Required revision (actionable): Add a dedicated “Ethics & Privacy” subsection clarifying: 
 
• Informed consent: Was explicit consent obtained? From whom, how, and with what participant 
information sheet? Was withdrawal possible? 
 
• Data type: Was raw webcam video recorded/stored, or only derived gaze coordinates / feature 
representations? 
 
• Anonymization: Face images/landmarks may be re-identifiable; specify anonymization/privacy 
measures (e.g., on-device processing, no raw video storage, pseudonymization). 
 
• Storage & access: Where are data stored, for how long, and who can access them? What 
security measures are used (encryption/access control)?

• Third-party transfer: If LLM/TTS or any external APIs are used, does any data leave the device? If 
yes, what payload is transmitted and how is it protected? If no, explicitly state that all processing 
is local and no data are transmitted to third parties. 
 
 
2) Data collection and participant protocol are not numerically auditable 
 
The manuscript reports session-level outcomes and some aggregate counts, but it does not 
clearly specify the number of unique participants, inclusion/exclusion criteria, participant 
profile, device variability, and environmental conditions. 
 
Missing: A reproducible study protocol at the level expected for empirical HCI/AI system papers. 
 
Required revision (actionable): Add a “Data Collection / Study Protocol” summary table 
including: 
 
• #participants (unique individuals) and minimal demographics summary (e.g., age range) 
 
• #sessions, session duration, reading material type/length, total paragraphs 
 
• device/screen info (resolution, screen size), camera type (built-in/external), basic conditions 
(approx. distance/lighting) 
 
• calibration procedure (9-point) and whether re-calibration/drift handling was performed 
 
 
3) Lack of webcam baseline and ablation studies 
 
While the paper motivates webcam tracking as a low-cost alternative to hardware eye trackers, it 
does not provide a quantitative comparison against at least one webcam-based baseline 
method (same family of solutions). A conceptual comparison table alone is not sufficient. 
 
Missing: “Proposed vs. baseline” evidence. 
 
Required revision (actionable): 
 
• Add at least one webcam gaze-tracking baseline (from literature or a reasonable open-source 
method) and compare under the same protocol: accuracy/stability/latency. 
 
• Provide minimal ablation: 
 
o KDE smoothing on vs. off 
 
o calibration variant (e.g., 5-point vs 9-point, or re-calibration strategy) 
 
o dwell-threshold strategy (single threshold vs. two-threshold/interval logic)

4) Metric definitions and ground-truth validation are unclear 
 
The manuscript reports metrics such as “focus score, ” “difficulty ratio, ” and “mean focus score, ” 
but their formal definitions, computation pipeline, and validation approach are not sufficiently 
specified. 
 
Missing: Clear metric definitions and reliability reporting. 
 
Required revision (actionable): 
 
• Provide explicit formulae + a worked example for “focus score” and “difficulty ratio. ” 
 
• Report at least dispersion/uncertainty (mean±std or 95% CI) and per-session/per-participant 
distributions. 
 
• If feasible, add a small-scale reference validation subset (task-based ground truth, 
comprehension questions, or a controlled validation protocol). At minimum, strengthen “Threats 
to Validity. ” 
 
 
 
5) LLM/TTS components are underspecified (model/service, operating mode, and data flow) 
 
The paper includes AI-assisted features such as LLM-based summarization and TTS-based 
reading, yet it does not specify the LLM/TTS provider/model, whether inference is local or cloud-
based, what data are transmitted/logged, and what safeguards are in place. 
 
Missing: Technical details and privacy/security implications of LLM/TTS usage. 
 
Required revision (actionable): 
 
• Specify the LLM/TTS provider/model name(s) and whether they run locally or via cloud APIs 
 
• If APIs are used: detail the payload (raw paragraph text? user identifiers?), 
masking/anonymization, and access controls 
 
• Describe prompt/output constraints and logging/retention policy (if any) 
 
 
 
6) “Real-time” claim requires end-to-end latency distribution and hardware specification 
 
The reported component-level performance values are helpful; however, for a strong “real-time” 
claim, the paper should report end-to-end latency (camera → processing → decision → UI update) 
and the test hardware used. 
 
Missing: End-to-end latency statistics and system configuration.
